As tropical climate become hotter and more unpredictable, architecture must shift toward climate-responsive design. The aim is not only comfort, but **resilience**: reducing heat gain, lowering energy demand, managing water, and maintaining livability during more extreme and erratic weather.

### Basic Concepts
- Reduce heat gain, especially from solar exposure, through double-skin facades, roof overhangs, and external shading devices.
- Prioritize natural ventilation and air movement, which are far more energy-efficient than compressor-based cooling. Cross ventilation and ceiling fans are essential.
- Use air conditioning selectively, focusing on smaller “refuge spaces” such as bedrooms or work areas during extreme heat.
- Generate on-site solar power to utilize Indonesia’s solar potential while improving resilience against outages and rising electricity costs.
- Manage rainwater through harvesting and infiltration strategies to address both droughts and increasingly intense storms.

#### Double-Skin Facade
- West facades receive intense afternoon sun, creating high radiant heat. Use a ventilated second skin to block and expel heat before it reaches the main wall.
- Keep the outer layer light and reflective (white paint, perforated metal, breezeblocks, louvers). Low thermal mass releases heat quickly instead of storing it into the night.
- Maintain airflow between layers; trapped air becomes a heat chamber. 
- Vegetation can also work as a living second skin, ideally rooted directly in the ground for greater drought resilience and lower maintenance.

#### Roof Shape & Overhangs
- Most tropical heat and rain enter through roofs and openings. Deep overhangs reduce solar gain and protect walls, windows, and vents from rain. Aim for overhang depth roughly equal to opening height (1:1 ratio), especially on west-facing sides.
- Use simple pitched roofs (e.g. atap pelana). Fewer joints and valleys mean fewer leak points during extreme rain.
- Avoid or minimize gutters above occupied spaces. External gutters leak less dangerously and are easier to inspect and repair.

#### Ventilated Cool Roof
- Roof surfaces can exceed 60°C under direct sun. A cool, ventilated roof reduces heat transfer into the house.
- Use reflective finishes, insulated ceilings, and double-layer roofs with ventilated air gaps. The outer layer blocks rain and sun; the air cavity vents accumulated heat outward.
- Ventilation is critical. Without airflow, the cavity traps hot humid air like an oven.
- Roof material matters. For example, UPVC generally transfers less heat than concrete tile, which performs better than bare metal roofing.

#### AC Compartmentalization (Thermal Zoning)
- Cooling entire tropical houses is energy-intensive. Instead, concentrate AC use in smaller “refuge spaces” such as bedrooms or work rooms.
- Smaller enclosed rooms cool faster, retain cooling longer, and reduce electricity demand.
- Keep AC rooms compact with lower ceilings and controllable openings. Seal enough to conserve cooling, but avoid excessive airtightness without dehumidification and ventilation, otherwise mold can develop quickly.

#### Solar Power
- Hot sunny days increase both cooling demand and solar production, making solar PV naturally aligned with tropical energy use.
- Use rooftop solar to directly offset daytime AC loads; batteries are optional.
- Solar panels can also function as shading canopies. Leave airflow beneath panels to reduce panel temperature, since cooler PV panels operate more efficiently.

#### Tall Ceilings & Stack Ventilation
- Hot air rises. Taller ceilings create a larger buffer above occupied zones, improving comfort without mechanical cooling.
- Combine high ceilings with upper vents and ceiling fans to drive stack ventilation: cooler air enters low, hot air exits high.
- Large rooms benefit most from tall ceilings; smaller rooms can remain lower and more energy-efficient.
- Provide shading to high vents to reduce rain intrusion while maintaining airflow.

#### Cross Ventilation
- Moving air increases evaporative cooling on the skin, making spaces feel significantly cooler even at the same temperature.
- Place openings across from each other to create pressure-driven airflow. Align major openings with prevailing winds where possible. But urban wind patterns are unpredictable and may shift over time. So a flexible design with multiple adjustable openings on different sides would be best.
- Openings should be controllable. During extremely hot or humid periods, unrestricted ventilation can worsen indoor comfort.
- Avoid blocking airflow paths with large furniture or partitions.

#### Elevated Ground Floor
- Raising the house protects living areas from flooding, damp ground conditions, and surface runoff. It also provides more area for rainwater infiltration.
- Airflow beneath the floor also helps reduce heat and moisture buildup.
- Even partial crawl spaces improve maintenance access and flood resilience while preserving ground infiltration.

#### Rainwater Harvesting & Infiltration
- Tropical storms dump large volumes of water quickly. Use biopores, permeable paving, gravel with Turfpave, and green areas to increase soil absorption and reduce flooding. Effectivity may depend on soil type and surrounding groundwater condition.
- Elevated rainwater tanks are most efficient in Indonesia: gravity-fed, easier to maintain, and less dependent on electric pumps.
- Ideal sequence: roof → gutter → first-flush filter → elevated tank → toilets, irrigation, or cleaning. All via gravity flow.

#### Maintenance-Friendly Design
- Tropical buildings deteriorate faster due to heat, humidity, UV exposure, and heavy rain. Design for replacement and maintenance access from the start.
- Include ceiling access panels, exposed inspection points, and modular facade systems.
- Use replaceable components and avoid details that permanently trap moisture, dirt, or debris.
