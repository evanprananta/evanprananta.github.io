Spending months at home during the COVID-19 lock down made me realize something. Traveling is overrated.

Nowadays, a lot of people see traveling as some kind of end goal in life. The ultimate cure for a boring, meaningless life. After spending four years on the opposite side of the world and traveling around Europe, I've found that it doesn't cure anything. At best, traveling is a painkiller we take to soothe the dull pain of the 'normal life'. Here's why.

## We don't learn as much as we think.

Most people pick and choose when it comes to traveling. And even if we try to travel as 'authentically' as possible, there's actually little we could learn during our travels.

The things we do during our travels are usually just an extension of our usual selves. The places we go to as tourists and travelers can only reveal very little about what's actually going on. Even natural destinations could hide a lot of their truth.

I went to Drachenfels in Königswinter several times during my time in Germany. I've come during great weather where I saw the shadows of the clouds rolling on the hills below. But I've also been there when it was misty and drizzling, when I couldn't see three meters in front of me. These contrasting experiences are the reward we can only get by staying long enough in one place. If I'd stayed for just a few days, I would've only known the place as either a heavenly throne or a mysterious old fortress.

## We could learn more by doing other things.

A lot of proponents of traveling would quote St. Augustine:

> The world is a book and those who do not travel read only one page.

This might've been true during *his* time, but nowadays, I'd argue that we can learn more while staying at home than while flying around.

The amount of information at our fingertips has been rising exponentially. We could literally see the other side of the world from the comfort of our desks. I even believe that the amount of information contained in books, blogs, and other media on the internet is now larger than what the physical world can offer. Compared to a day of browsing the internet, traveling a much less efficient way of learning things.

Sure, there are pitfalls when it comes to the internet. Distractions and empty entertainment are abound. But traveling is also rife with potential problems. Arriving at the wrong place and the wrong time (European small city, Sunday) might mean there's nothing to do. Pickpockets, spicy food, and racist remarks can easily blind us from the wonders we came across.

And sure, there are some things we could only learn by traveling. Navigating different cultures and airports, knowing how it feels to be a minority, knowing what to pack, etc. But the same thing could be said for fishing, gardening, and any other kind of activity. Just because traveling can teach us *some*thing doesn't mean it teaches us more or better.

## We learn by doing, not just experiencing.

The best things are only truly learned through practice. We might *experience* new things while traveling. But until we actually spend time *doing* and practicing them, the experience itself isn't worth much. (Except as a story to tell.) Visiting a different coffee shop every week doesn't make you a better coffee maker if you never try to make one yourself.

Traveling might feel fulfilling and even productive at times. We see and experience new things, and we get tired from it. Those are the *symptoms* of a fruitful time. But as I've found again and again, the high we get from this surface level novelty is a hollow one. After a while the newfound confidence will be eroded by our old routines, and we'll be left with nothing to hold on to beside another story.

To go one step further, traveling a lot might even be detrimental to growing as a person. Learning is best done by deliberate practice - repetition and intentionally trying to get better. From my experience, a stable environment and routine is a great foundation to do so. Getting better at something is so much harder to do when you also need to get better at riding trains and buying food in a foreign place. The boring routine we try to escape from might actually provide the stability needed for deliberate practice. Maybe that's why [many creative people's lives](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/15799151-daily-rituals) were so structured.

## Adventure is everywhere.

Whenever I feel bored, stuck or trapped in a place, I've found that novelty and inspiration could always be found nearby. There's a ton we could do to see and experience new things in our daily lives.

For example, I've tried taking the first train of the day. I rode a bus I took every day, to its end destination. I went outside at night while it's raining and walked across a pitch black field nearby. Something as simple as taking a different route home could be more of an adventure than a day-long flight. In the end, *doing new things* is what actually gives us new perspectives. Doing the same things in different places doesn't.

---

There's nothing inherently wrong with traveling. I've enjoyed it myself from time to time (before COVID-19). But there *is* something wrong with attributing some kind of superiority or moral value to it, when in fact, traveling is just another form of consumption.
