As far as I remember, I've always been struggling with nighttime. There were times when I dread it, but mostly, my mind likes to get hyperactive after the dark. I've always thought that this is simply because I'm a 'night owl', that my body is made to function this way.

During my college years, several short-term experiments proved that it's not as simple as that. I can wake up easily and my mind can function clearly in the early morning as long as I get enough sleep. Even the cold and darkness during winter didn't seem to matter much as long as I have a routine ready. This experience, coupled with the growing detriments of working late into the night, pushed me to try out a new method of planning my days.

## How the to-do-list failed me.

For a long time, I've planned my days around to-do-lists. Some to-dos are weeks long, some are specific to the day. While this worked okay, I've noticed that it is not the most accurate and effective method of planning.

### 1. I overestimate how many things I could get done in a day.

On the days when there is more to do, I'd dump everything into the list. The lack of planning made it much easier to list the things, but harder to actually get them all done.

### 2. It doesn't help me with mental clarity.

The planning on when to do one vs the other happened only in my head, or after I finished one task. This meant that I had to continuously think about what to do next, even if I batch some tasks together. And when I don't feel like doing the next thing, it leads to the next problem.

### 3. I tend to procrastinate on the harder things on my do-do-list.

Doing the easy stuffs - shopping during summer, cleaning the room - is a breeze. But when it comes down to working on something hard and boring, the to-do-list doesn't provide much incentive to get them done. It actually allowed me the space to procrastinate until I don't have enough time to do it that day. When faced with uncertainty of what to do and some boring tasks, I often picked the easiest route of watching YouTube.

### 4. The to-do-list promotes short bursts of work, but not consistent routines.

I have two hypotheses on why this was the case for me. First is that the to-do-list creates external rewards to each task, which is the sense of achievement you get after crossing off an item. This works great for small things that take little time and effort to do, because you get a chain of dopamine boost for finishing them. But for tasks that take longer, this positive effect is more or less negated. This might also explain why I tend to delay working on the bigger tasks on my list.

The act of making the list might also contribute to the lack of consistency. Writing the same things for multiple days in a row feels demotivating. You get reminded of that particular failure, and you feel less capable every time you have to write down that same thing.

## How I schedule my day with time blocking.

There is of course no one way to do scheduling. But these are the things that I found worked well for me. I've experimented with some other patterns, such as scheduling the whole week at once and making the schedule in the morning. So far, the following steps had worked the best for me for several reasons which I will also explain.

### 1. Planning

I do the actual scheduling at night, before I go to bed. For me, going to sleep is a much bigger problem than getting up in the morning. Therefore, the ritual of making plans at night helps me maintain a certain consistency that helps with falling asleep.
For that same reason, I do this low intensity activity with only pen and paper, and a dim night light. Planning at night gives me a reason and motivation to have screen-free time before bed.
Besides doing it as a nighttime routine, I do the planning every day because it allows for faster iteration and more accurate plans. Each day is structured in a similar way, but the finer points on what to do and how long things take often differ a lot within the week. There is a certain sense of achievement when the day goes the way I planned it, and I find that impossible to achieve without revising the schedule every day.

### 2. Execution

This is simple. I wake up, and I execute the plan. The more interesting thing is when I need to do something that was previously unplanned.

### 3. Revision

A lot of unexpected things can happen even in the most boring days. And when we need to react to the important things among them, we simply have to.
But what if that unexpected thing comes from within? This could be a misappropriation of time during planning or problems during execution. I personally find these instances to be the most valuable learning moments from this whole time blocking habit. When I do something that I didn't expect, I simply write it down on the same piece of paper that contains my plan.
In these short notes, I try to explain how and why this thing happened. Do I need to be faster? Do I need to allocate more time? Am I feeling unmotivated and sleepy around 2pm? I write down my thoughts and then continue on with what I was doing until I get back on track with the rest of the daily plan.

### 4. Review

The review is, for me, the most valuable part of time blocking or scheduling in general. As I hinted in 'revision', a time-blocked schedule makes it much more apparent when I'm doing something amiss. And the notes I make every time that happens get collected and reviewed during the night.
I do the review just before I make the next plan. This way, it also becomes part of the buffer between work and sleep. Another benefit to reviewing and planning at night is that I can still remember everything that happened during the day. Even if I failed to note down some things, I can usually still recall them at night.
Some of the lessons from the review will directly affect how I make the next schedule. I might find that I need more time for certain tasks, and less than I thought for the others. On the other hand, some of the more valuable lessons are less immediate. These ones are often the more valuable lessons for the long-term.

## Things I've learned from my time blocking experiment.

### Increased motivation during low-energy periods.

For me personally, time blocking helps a lot during afternoon. Especially after a big lunch, energy level and motivation are usually at their lowest point in the day. While a nap might fix this, the motivation usually doesn't return for a few hours. Having a scheduled thing to do helps me to keep moving and not get drowsy for the rest of the day.

### Increased mental clarity and reducing decision fatigue.

Time blocking reduces decision making during the day, and in turn allows for more mental clarity. Besides helping with better focus and mental performance, having clarity also adds a sense of control. This sense of control and better performance will positively reinforce each other through the day, giving you bigger and bigger wins like some video game combos.

### Weighing the urgent vs important.

Without any well-defined structure in the day, I don't have a frame of reference for evaluating distractions. Everything novel feels important, and I get distracted easily. With a time-blocked schedule, I can ask myself, "Is this emergency really more urgent and important than what I have planned?" If something important comes up, I can easily drop my plans. But I would have to make a conscious choice to do so, instead of letting the urgent distractions automatically win.

### Task-batching.

I don't schedule in blocks of time smaller than 15 minutes. If I need to get small tasks done, time blocking naturally encourages creating a 15 minutes span to group them together and get them done in a go. This approach prevents the small tasks from spreading themselves throughout the day. In turn, batching them together improves and protects my focus on the big tasks.

### Planned leisure time.

Some people say that planned leisure is less enjoyable. And studies certainly have showed that that is the case. But in my experience, you can get more relaxed and enjoy things more because you know the time is totally dedicated to fun activities. This is particularly useful for those who often feel guilty about taking time to relax. While nothing beats spontaneous fun, planned leisure is certainly better than guilty pleasure.

### Self-evaluation and daily review.

As I've stated before, for me this is the most impactful aspect of time blocking. My time-blocked schedule provides a framework upon which I can look at how and why I spend my days. Without these pre-planned time blocks, the day becomes one big blur, and I couldn't be sure whether or not I've spent it well.
Time blocking also promotes self evaluation because I want to make a more realistic schedule for the next day. Rather than thinking about life lessons as some abstract dreams for the far future, time blocking makes them seem like actionable points.

***

Time blocking is no magic bullet, and I'm a bit hesitant that this piece might read like some kind of productivity hack. But this one has actually been very useful for me. Time blocking is a commitment device. It is a structure that pushes me to go through each day as I ideally envisioned it the night before, and to take willpower of the present self out of the equation.
